How to Choose Your First Pickleball Paddle

Updated July 2026 · 8 min read · For absolute beginners

Walking into a pickleball shop for the first time is overwhelming. Dozens of brands, weird words like "twist weight" and "core thickness," and price tags from $30 to $300+. The good news: your first paddle doesn't need to be expensive or complicated. It needs to help you keep the ball in play while you learn the game. This guide breaks the choice down into five things that actually matter — and there's a free matcher tool at the bottom that turns this into a shortlist for you.

1. Weight: start in the middle

Most adult paddles weigh between 7.5 and 8.5 ounces. Here's the simple rule:

  • Lighter (7.5–7.9 oz): easier to control, less tiring, better while you're building technique.
  • Heavier (8.2–8.5 oz): more power, but it fatigues your arm and punishes sloppy contact.

For a first paddle, aim for 7.6–8.2 oz. You get enough mass to drive the ball without fighting your own wrist. Skip anything labeled "max power" or "lightweight" — both extremes hurt beginners.

2. Core thickness: thicker is friendlier

The core is the honeycomb (or foam) inside the paddle face. Thickness usually runs 13–16mm:

  • 16mm (thick): softer feel, more forgiveness, easier touch at the net. Best for beginners.
  • 14mm (thin): more pop and power, but less forgiving on off-center hits.

If this is your first paddle, choose 16mm. You'll thank yourself during long rallies.

3. Shape: widebody is the safe choice

Paddle faces come in three shapes:

  • Widebody: the biggest sweet spot, most predictable. Start here.
  • Standard: a balance of sweet spot and reach.
  • Elongated: extra reach and power, but a smaller sweet spot — save it until your contact is consistent.

4. Grip size: match your hand

Grip circumference usually runs 4.0"–4.5". A quick test:

  • Small hands: start at 4.0".
  • Most adults: 4.25".
  • Large hands: 4.5".

You can always build a grip up with an overgrip, but you can't easily shrink one. When in doubt, go slightly smaller.

5. Budget: $80–$130 is the sweet spot

You do not need a $250 paddle to learn. In fact, buying a pro-level thermoformed paddle too early often slows your progress because it rewards technique you haven't built yet. A solid carbon or polymer paddle in the $80–$130 range will last you through your first year and still perform well.

Beginner mistake we see most: spending $180 on a raw carbon paddle before mechanics are developed enough to notice the difference.

A simple checklist to take to the store

  1. Weight 7.6–8.2 oz
  2. Core 16mm
  3. Widebody shape
  4. Grip 4.0"–4.5" (match your hand)
  5. Budget $80–$130
  6. USAPA-approved (if you'll enter tournaments)
